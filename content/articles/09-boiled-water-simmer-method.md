Food welded itself to your skillet and you are standing at the sink wondering how hard you can scrub before the seasoning dies. Stop. Put the scrub pad down.

**Pour a cup of water into the pan, put it back on the heat, and let it boil for a minute or two.** The stuck food lifts off with a gentle scrape — no force, no steel wool, no damage to your seasoning. This is the single most useful cleaning move in cast iron, and it takes about two minutes.

I use it weekly on pans I've owned for years. Here is exactly how to do it, when it is not enough, and the one mistake that will warp your pan.

## The method in 4 steps

1. **Return the pan to medium heat.** If you just cooked in it, it is probably still warm — that is fine.
2. **Add one cup of water and bring to a boil.** Let it bubble for 1–2 minutes. You will see the stuck bits start to float.
3. **Scrape gently with a wooden spatula** as it bubbles. The food releases with almost no pressure. A flat-edged wooden spoon works too — avoid metal on a seasoned surface unless you must.
4. **Rinse with hot water and dry on the stove** over low heat for a minute. Never air-dry cast iron; that is how rust starts.

## Why it works

Stuck food is carbonized oil and protein mechanically bonded to the seasoning. Boiling water does two things at once: it re-liquifies the oil holding the bond together, and the bubbling action physically agitates the debris loose. You are not dissolving the seasoning — polymerized oil is a plastic-like layer that a two-minute simmer cannot touch. You are just softening the glue under the food.

That is why this method is safe for even a young, fragile seasoning, where steel wool or aggressive scrubbing would set you back weeks.

## When to escalate

The simmer handles food residue. It does **not** handle these — and pretending it will just wastes an evening:

| Problem | What you actually need |
|---|---|
| Burnt-on grease film (sticky, brown-black, covers the whole cooking surface) | A restoration-level strip, not a simmer <!-- TODO-LINK: /restoration/ (#38) when live --> |
| Rust anywhere on the pan | Rust removal first; cleaning second <!-- TODO-LINK: /troubleshooting/ (#29) when live --> |
| Food sticking *during* cooking, every time | That is a heat-management or seasoning problem, not a cleaning problem |

> **Warning:** If boiling for 3 minutes changes nothing, stop boiling. You are now just evaporating water. Move to the escalation that matches the problem in the table above.

## What NOT to do

- **Do not plunge a ripping-hot pan under cold water.** Thermal shock is how cast iron warps. The pan should be warm or medium-hot — never straight-off-the-flame screaming hot when the water hits.
- **Do not reach for steel wool on a well-seasoned pan.** It will absolutely remove the stuck food. It will also remove the seasoning you spent months building. Reserve abrasive stripping for restorations.
- **Do not soak the pan for hours.** A long soak invites rust at any scratch or thin spot. Two minutes of boiling beats two hours of soaking every time.

## The bonus: deglaze and eat it

If what is stuck to the pan is browned bits (fond) rather than burnt black — that is flavor, not failure. Splash in a little stock, wine, or water while the pan is hot, scrape it up, and you have the base of a pan sauce. ## Frequently asked questions

### Does boiling water damage cast iron seasoning?

No. A one-to-two minute simmer stays well below the temperatures that harm polymerized oil, and the water never touches the seasoning long enough to matter.

### Can I use this method every time I clean?

You can, but you should not need to. If food sticks this often, the problem is usually heat management or a young seasoning — fix that instead of boiling after every meal.

### What if boiling water alone does not lift the food?

For truly carbonized build-up, move up to a chainmail scrubber on a warm (not ripping-hot) pan, or for heavy burnt-on grease, a full restoration strip.
